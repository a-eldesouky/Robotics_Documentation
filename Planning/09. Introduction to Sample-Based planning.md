# Sample-Based Planning

## Introduction

The path planning examples studied in discrete or combinatorial planning are usually simple and limited in size. Most of them are two-dimensional examples, where the robot moves on a grid or map using graph-search algorithms such as BFS, Dijkstra, Uniform Cost Search, or A*.

This simplification is useful because many real robots can be reasonably modeled in two dimensions. For example, a vacuum robot moving inside a house does not need a complex three-dimensional representation of the world. It mainly translates and rotates on the flat 2D plane of the floor.

In that case, the robot configuration can often be represented as:

[
q = (x, y, \theta)
]

where:

* (x) is the robot position along the horizontal axis,
* (y) is the robot position along the vertical axis,
* (\theta) is the robot orientation.

However, not all robotics problems can be simplified this way. Some robots operate in three-dimensional environments. Others have six degrees of freedom or more. In these cases, path planning becomes much more challenging.

Although it may still be possible to apply classical graph-search algorithms, their efficiency becomes a serious limitation. Performing a complete discretization of the entire configuration space and then applying graph search may be too computationally expensive.

To solve larger and higher-dimensional path planning problems, a different family of algorithms is used: **sample-based path planning**.

Instead of discretizing the entire configuration space, sample-based planners randomly sample the space and attempt to build a useful representation from those samples. The goal is not to represent every possible configuration exactly, but to collect enough valid samples to approximate the structure of the free space.

This lesson focuses on two major ideas:

1. **Sample-based path planning**, which handles large and high-dimensional spaces more efficiently.
2. **Probabilistic path planning**, which explicitly considers uncertainty in robot motion when planning paths.

---

## Why Discrete Planning Becomes Inefficient

Discrete planning works well when the configuration space is small. The planner can divide the environment into cells or states, connect neighboring states, and search for a path.

However, this approach becomes difficult when the number of dimensions increases.

The main reason is that the complexity of the planning problem grows exponentially with the number of dimensions in the configuration space.

This is known as the **curse of dimensionality**.

---

## Increased Dimensionality

Consider a two-dimensional 8-connected grid.

In an 8-connected space, each cell can move to 8 neighboring cells:

* up,
* down,
* left,
* right,
* and the four diagonal directions.

So, in 2D:

[
3^2 - 1 = 8
]

The formula for the number of neighboring successors in an (n)-dimensional grid is:

[
3^n - 1
]

The reason is that in each dimension, the planner has three possibilities:

* move in the negative direction,
* stay in the same coordinate,
* move in the positive direction.

So for (n) dimensions, there are:

[
3^n
]

possible combinations.

One of these combinations is the case where the robot does not move at all, so it is removed:

[
3^n - 1
]

### Example: 3D Space

For a three-dimensional space:

[
3^3 - 1 = 27 - 1 = 26
]

So every node in a 3D grid may have 26 successors.

This is already much larger than the 8 successors in a 2D grid.

### Example: 6D Space

For a 6-dimensional space:

[
3^6 - 1 = 729 - 1 = 728
]

A 6-DOF robot arm may therefore have hundreds of possible neighboring configurations for each expanded node if discretized directly.

This makes graph search much more expensive.

---

## High-Dimensional Robots

Many robotic systems naturally have high-dimensional configuration spaces.

A robotic arm used in a pick-and-place task may have 6 degrees of freedom. Its configuration may be represented as:

[
q = (\theta_1, \theta_2, \theta_3, \theta_4, \theta_5, \theta_6)
]

where each (\theta_i) is a joint angle.

If multiple 6-DOF arms work in the same workspace, the planning problem becomes even harder. The planner must avoid:

* collisions with the environment,
* self-collisions,
* collisions between robots,
* invalid joint configurations,
* unreachable poses.

For two 6-DOF arms, the combined configuration space may become 12-dimensional:

[
q = (\theta_1, ..., \theta_6, \phi_1, ..., \phi_6)
]

For humanoid robots, the number of degrees of freedom can be even larger. A humanoid robot may need to coordinate legs, arms, torso, balance, and contact constraints at the same time.

Such problems may take an intolerably long time to solve using a purely combinatorial approach.

---

## Complete Discretization Becomes Too Costly

In combinatorial planning, the planner typically discretizes the whole configuration space.

If each dimension is divided into (k) discrete values, and the robot has (n) dimensions, the total number of states becomes:

[
k^n
]

For example, if a 6-DOF arm has only 100 discretized values per joint:

[
100^6 = 1,000,000,000,000
]

That is one trillion possible configurations.

Even before searching for a path, simply representing the full discretized configuration space can be too expensive.

This is the central reason sample-based planning is needed.

---

## Constrained Dynamics

High dimensionality is not the only difficulty. Another major challenge comes from robots with constrained dynamics.

A simple grid planner may assume that the robot can move freely from one neighboring cell to another. But many real robots cannot move in arbitrary directions.

A car is a clear example.

A car can:

* move forward,
* move backward,
* turn left or right with a limited turning radius.

But a car cannot move directly sideways.

This means that even if a nearby position is geometrically close, the car may not be able to reach it directly.

---

## Car-Like Motion Constraints

A car-like robot has motion constraints caused by its steering geometry.

A simplified car model can be described as:

[
\dot{x} = v \cos(\theta)
]

[
\dot{y} = v \sin(\theta)
]

[
\dot{\theta} = \frac{v}{L} \tan(\delta)
]

where:

* (x) and (y) describe the vehicle position,
* (\theta) is the heading angle,
* (v) is the velocity,
* (L) is the wheelbase,
* (\delta) is the steering angle.

This model shows that the next motion of the vehicle depends on its heading, velocity, and steering angle.

The vehicle cannot choose arbitrary changes in (x), (y), and (\theta). Its motion must follow the allowed dynamics.

---

## Turning Radius and Velocity

For a car, the safe turning radius depends on motion constraints and velocity.

At low speed, the car can turn more sharply.

At high speed, sharp turns may be unsafe or physically impossible.

This means that path planning may need to consider not only position and orientation, but also derivatives of the state, such as:

* velocity,
* acceleration,
* steering rate,
* curvature.

Therefore, planning for a car-like robot is more complex than planning for a point robot on a grid.

---

## Holonomic and Non-Holonomic Systems

Robotic systems can be classified into two important categories:

1. **Holonomic systems**
2. **Non-holonomic systems**

This classification is important because it affects how difficult the path planning problem becomes.

---

### Holonomic Systems

A holonomic system is a system where every constraint depends only on the current pose and time.

The constraints do not depend on derivatives such as velocity or acceleration.

In simpler terms, a holonomic robot can move directly in the directions allowed by its configuration space.

For example, an omnidirectional mobile robot can move:

* forward,
* backward,
* sideways,
* diagonally,
* and rotate independently.

This makes planning easier because the robot has fewer motion restrictions.

---

### Non-Holonomic Systems

A non-holonomic system has constraints that depend on derivatives of the state.

These constraints involve quantities such as:

* velocity,
* acceleration,
* angular velocity,
* steering rate.

A car is non-holonomic because it cannot move sideways. Its future motion depends on its current heading and velocity.

For example, even if the goal is directly to the side of the car, the car cannot move laterally into that position. It must follow a curved maneuver.

This makes path planning more difficult because the planner must produce paths that are not only collision-free, but also physically executable.

---

## Why Non-Holonomic Planning Is Harder

For a holonomic robot, a path may be valid if it avoids obstacles.

For a non-holonomic robot, obstacle avoidance is not enough.

The path must also respect the robot’s motion constraints.

A path for a car-like robot must avoid:

* impossible sideways motion,
* turns sharper than the minimum turning radius,
* sudden orientation changes,
* velocity-dependent unsafe turns,
* dynamically infeasible maneuvers.

So the planner must reason about both:

1. **where the robot should go**, and
2. **how the robot can physically get there**.

This is why non-holonomic planning is more difficult than ordinary grid-based planning.

---

## Why Sample-Based Planning Helps

Sample-based planning is introduced to handle problems where complete discretization is too expensive.

Instead of building the entire configuration space, the planner samples configurations from the space.

A sample may represent:

* a 2D robot pose,
* a 3D robot pose,
* a 6-DOF arm joint configuration,
* a humanoid body configuration,
* a multi-robot combined state.

The planner then checks whether these samples are valid and tries to connect them.

The goal is to create a useful approximation of the free configuration space without explicitly enumerating every possible state.

---

## Core Idea of Sample-Based Planning

The central idea is:

> Do not discretize everything. Sample enough of the space to discover a feasible path.

A typical sample-based planner does the following:

1. Randomly sample configurations from the configuration space.
2. Reject samples that are in collision or invalid.
3. Keep samples that lie in free space.
4. Try to connect nearby valid samples.
5. Use the resulting graph or tree to find a path from start to goal.

This approach is especially useful when the configuration space is large or high-dimensional.

---

## Why This Is a Practical Compromise

Sample-based planning does not usually provide the same strict guarantees as exact combinatorial planning.

Instead, it accepts a practical compromise:

* It avoids complete discretization.
* It reduces memory requirements.
* It scales better to high-dimensional spaces.
* It can find feasible paths faster.
* It can handle complex robot configurations.

The planner may not perfectly represent the space, but it can often represent it well enough to solve the planning problem.

---

## Algorithms Introduced in This Section

Two major sample-based planning algorithms are commonly introduced first:

### 1. Probabilistic Roadmap Method

The **Probabilistic Roadmap Method**, or **PRM**, samples the configuration space and builds a graph called a roadmap.

It is especially useful for static environments where the same roadmap can be reused for multiple planning queries.

### 2. Rapidly-Exploring Random Tree

The **Rapidly-Exploring Random Tree**, or **RRT**, builds a tree from the start configuration and expands it toward unexplored regions of the space.

It is especially useful for single-query planning problems and high-dimensional spaces.

---

## Parameter Tuning

Sample-based planners depend heavily on their parameters.

Important parameters include:

* number of samples,
* sampling distribution,
* connection radius,
* number of nearest neighbors,
* step size,
* collision-checking resolution,
* goal bias,
* maximum planning time.

Tuning these parameters affects:

* planning speed,
* success rate,
* path quality,
* memory usage,
* ability to pass through narrow spaces.

For example, increasing the number of samples improves the chance of finding a path, but also increases computation time.

A larger connection radius may improve graph connectivity, but it also increases the number of collision checks.

A larger RRT step size may explore faster, but may skip important narrow regions.

---

## Practical Summary

Discrete and combinatorial planning are powerful for small and low-dimensional problems, especially 2D grid-based environments.

However, they become inefficient when:

* the configuration space has many dimensions,
* the robot has many degrees of freedom,
* multiple robots share the same space,
* the robot has constrained dynamics,
* non-holonomic motion must be respected,
* complete discretization becomes too expensive.

Sample-based planning addresses these challenges by avoiding full discretization. Instead, it samples the configuration space and builds an approximate representation that is often sufficient to find a feasible path.

The key motivation is simple:

> In high-dimensional robotics, it is often better to sample intelligently than to discretize exhaustively.

This is why sample-based planning becomes essential for complex robots such as robotic arms, car-like robots, humanoids, drones, and multi-robot systems.
